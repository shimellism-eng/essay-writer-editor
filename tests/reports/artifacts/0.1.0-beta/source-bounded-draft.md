# The City Library Should Keep Its Fine-Free Policy

The City Library should keep its fine-free policy for children’s accounts, but it should treat the policy as promising rather than proven. The first-year review gives two substantial reasons for continuing: many surveyed account holders reported that fear of fees had delayed them from returning an item, and the number of overdue items returned increased during the pilot year. Yet the review cannot show that removing fees caused that rise, and it leaves an important question about replacement costs unanswered. These limits justify closer evaluation, not immediate reversal. On the evidence available, continuing the policy while improving how its effects are measured is the more defensible choice.

The survey provides the clearest evidence that late fees can obstruct the behaviour the library wants to encourage. Of 300 account holders surveyed, 180 said that fear of fees had delayed them from returning an item (City Library, 2025, pp. 4–7). That is three fifths of the respondents, not a marginal group. Their answers do not prove what every account holder would do, but they identify a specific barrier among the people surveyed. The report’s conclusion captures the significance of this finding: ‘Removing fines lowered one barrier to re-engagement’ (City Library, 2025, pp. 4–7). If fear of a fee makes some people postpone a return, maintaining that fee may work against the aim of getting those items back. Removing it gives those account holders one less reason to delay.

The return figures point in the same direction. Overdue item returns rose from 1,000 to 1,200 in the first year, an increase of 200 returns (City Library, 2025, pp. 4–7). This is an encouraging outcome because it occurred after late fees were removed from children’s accounts. It would nevertheless be inaccurate to present the change as proof that the policy worked. The report states that other outreach programmes were running during the same year and does not isolate the effect of the fine-free policy (City Library, 2025, pp. 4–7). The increase could reflect the policy, the outreach programmes, or a combination of both. A sound argument for continuation must preserve that uncertainty.

That uncertainty creates the strongest case against making the policy permanent without further scrutiny. The review also gives no estimate of replacement costs (City Library, 2025, pp. 4–7). Consequently, it cannot show whether the returned items offset, exceed, or fall short of any costs associated with items that were not returned. This missing information matters to the decision. However, it does not demonstrate that the policy is too costly; it shows only that the report cannot answer the cost question. Reinstating fines on the basis of an unmeasured cost would therefore go beyond the evidence just as surely as declaring the pilot an unqualified success.

The absence of rural-library data is another genuine limit, but it should be interpreted narrowly. The review offers no basis for claiming that the same results would occur in rural libraries (City Library, 2025, pp. 4–7). The decision at issue, however, concerns the City Library. A lack of rural evidence restricts wider generalisation; it does not cancel the local survey responses or the recorded increase in overdue returns. The scope of the policy should also remain clear: the pilot removed late fees from children’s accounts, so the findings cannot automatically be extended to every type of account.

The library should therefore retain the fine-free policy while making the next stage answer the questions the first year could not. A further review should distinguish the policy’s effects from those of concurrent outreach, continue recording overdue returns, and estimate replacement costs. It should also keep its claims limited to the accounts and setting actually studied. This approach takes the favourable evidence seriously without exaggerating it. The pilot revealed a reported barrier and coincided with 200 more overdue returns, while the case for reversal remains unsupported by cost data. Keeping the policy, with stronger evaluation, is the decision best matched to the evidence.

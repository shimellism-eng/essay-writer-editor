# Essay structures and natural flow

Use structure to carry the argument, not to make every essay look symmetrical. The prompt, evidence, genre, and writer's decisions should determine the shape.

## Build an argument before arranging paragraphs

Define:

- the question the essay answers;
- the thesis or controlling idea;
- the sequence of claims needed to establish it;
- the evidence available for each claim;
- plausible counterarguments or limits;
- what follows if the thesis is right.

A thesis should make a meaningful choice. “There are advantages and disadvantages” usually names a topic rather than advancing an argument.

## Give paragraphs different jobs

A paragraph may:

- frame a problem or define a term;
- advance a claim;
- present evidence;
- analyse why the evidence matters;
- distinguish two similar ideas;
- test a counterargument;
- qualify the thesis;
- connect causes and consequences;
- pause on a revealing example;
- establish an implication.

Not every paragraph needs the same internal order. Keep the relation between claim, evidence, and analysis clear without forcing identical topic sentences, transitions, or closing lines.

## Choose a structure that fits

### Argumentative

Establish the dispute, state a defensible thesis, develop the strongest reasons, address a serious counterargument, and end with the consequence of the argument. Do not hide inconvenient evidence.

### Analytical

Define the object and question of analysis, choose an interpretive lens, examine evidence closely, test alternative readings, and show what the analysis reveals. Summary should support analysis rather than replace it.

### Expository

Organise by the reader's questions, process, causes, categories, or chronology. Explain terms at the point of need and distinguish settled information from uncertainty.

### Compare and contrast

Compare on meaningful shared criteria. Avoid writing two unrelated mini-essays followed by a superficial list of similarities. Explain what the comparison changes or clarifies.

### Reflective or narrative

Use only experiences the writer supplied. Connect scene, observation, reflection, and change without inventing biographical texture or spelling out every emotion.

### Application or personal statement

Build around supported choices, experiences, and values. Prefer one consequential example over a résumé in paragraph form. Never manufacture hardship, motivation, achievement, or personal growth.

## Diagnose mechanical shape

| Symptom | Better move |
|---|---|
| Every paragraph follows the same template | Let each paragraph end when its distinct job is complete |
| The point is stated, paraphrased, and summarised | Keep the clearest version and trust the reader |
| Claims remain universally vague | Add a supported name, case, object, action, number, or boundary |
| Every idea resolves neatly | Preserve a real tension, limit, or unanswered implication |
| Transitions announce every move | Use logical sequence, reference, contrast, or a clean break |
| The essay has no discernible thinker | Restore evidence-based judgements, priorities, and stakes |
| Examples feel interchangeable | Use source-provided detail that changes the reasoning |
| Each section ends with a slogan | End on the actual finding or consequence |
| The conclusion repeats the introduction | Extend, qualify, decide, or show what now follows |

Do not inject irrelevant tangents, errors, slang, fragments, or randomness to make prose appear human. An aside, time shift, or uneven paragraph belongs only when it improves meaning, pace, or voice.

## Use supported specificity

- Name real entities when the material supports them.
- Keep numbers attached to what they count.
- Prefer one revealing detail to several decorative ones.
- Distinguish observation, source claim, inference, and recommendation.
- Narrow a claim when greater specificity would require invention.
- Keep absence of evidence separate from evidence of absence.

A concrete falsehood is worse than an honest general statement.

## Create rhythm without gimmicks

- Let important qualifications take the space they need.
- Mix sentence and paragraph lengths according to thought and emphasis.
- Break repetitive openings, triads, mirrored sections, and serial conclusions when they feel mechanical.
- Use contractions, questions, fragments, or direct address only when they fit the writer and assignment.
- Prefer a natural repetition of a key term to a chain of inaccurate synonyms.
- Read prose aloud mentally and repair accidental cadence or breathless clauses.

Do not mechanically remove em dashes or any other punctuation. Punctuation is a choice about sense and cadence, not evidence of authorship.

## Open with substance

Useful openings may:

- state the problem precisely;
- begin with a supported scene or example;
- expose a tension;
- define a contested term;
- overturn a common assumption;
- ask the exact question the essay will answer.

Avoid generic claims about “today's world,” dictionary definitions with no analytical purpose, and inflated promises about what the essay will prove.

## End at the right moment

A conclusion may:

- show the consequence of the argument;
- resolve or sharpen the opening tension;
- identify a decision;
- qualify the thesis after testing it;
- connect the analysis to a broader but supported implication;
- leave the reader with a precise remaining question.

Do not repeat the whole essay, add new unsupported evidence, or announce importance a second time.

## Reconsider formulaic language

Phrases such as “it is important to note,” “delve into,” “a testament to,” “multifaceted,” “tapestry,” and “in conclusion” often carry little meaning. Replace them when the sentence becomes more precise, but do not maintain a banned-word list. A common phrase can still be the natural choice in context.

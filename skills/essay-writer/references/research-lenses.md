# Five-lens research pass

Use this process for research-led writing, contested claims, strategy, explainers, and decisions where a single prompt or consensus summary could create tunnel vision. Treat the roles as analytical lenses, not as real people who were consulted.

Do not attribute this workflow or any claimed effectiveness to Stanford, STORM, Claude, or another source unless the attribution has been verified. Do not repeat precise performance or time-saving claims without evidence.

Before external research, consider whether the user's essay, notes, or unpublished sources contain sensitive material. Search with the narrowest necessary query and do not upload private text to another service without the user's permission.

## Evidence rules

- Prefer primary research, official records, standards, and direct data for factual claims.
- Use high-quality reviews to understand a field, then inspect decisive underlying studies when possible.
- Record dates, jurisdictions, populations, definitions, and material limitations.
- Track the referent, unit, numerator, denominator, and time period for every important number. Different counts are not contradictory merely because their values differ.
- Distinguish what a source says from the writer's inference.
- Match citations to the exact nearby claim. Never cite a search snippet as evidence.
- Keep direct quotations exact and short. Mark translations and paraphrases honestly.
- Search for disconfirming evidence as deliberately as supporting evidence.
- Describe claims without an available source as unverified in the current material. Call them false or unsupported only when the evidence justifies that stronger conclusion.
- Prefer sources the reader can inspect. If access is restricted, say so rather than implying the full source was reviewed.

## Pass 1: Five-perspective scan

Investigate the topic through all five lenses:

1. **Practitioner:** What happens in day-to-day use? Which constraints, workarounds, failure modes, and tacit knowledge disappear from abstract accounts?
2. **Academic:** What does credible research support? How strong are the methods, samples, replications, and effect sizes? Where does the literature conflict with popular belief or with itself?
3. **Skeptic:** What is the strongest good-faith case against the prevailing view? Which assumptions, missing comparisons, or unfalsifiable claims weaken it?
4. **Incentives:** Who benefits financially, institutionally, politically, or reputationally from each narrative? Which funding, business models, career incentives, or selection effects shape what gets said?
5. **Historian:** What earlier cases genuinely resemble this one? What happened next, and where does the analogy break down?

If tools or browsing are available and the task needs current or external evidence, research the lenses with appropriate primary and authoritative sources. Do not simulate evidence from memory.

## Pass 2: Contradiction map

Map the important disagreements before drafting:

| Claim | Evidence for | Evidence against | Hidden assumption | Confidence | What would change the view? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Distinguish:

- factual disagreement from different value judgments;
- absence of evidence from evidence of absence;
- correlation from causation;
- expert consensus from institutional or commercial repetition;
- a real historical precedent from a superficial analogy.

## Pass 3: Synthesis

Write a synthesis that:

- states where the lenses converge;
- names the central unresolved conflicts;
- weights evidence by quality rather than counting viewpoints;
- separates observation, inference, and recommendation;
- explains relevant incentives without reducing every belief to money;
- uses historical parallels with explicit limits;
- preserves uncertainty and identifies the decision that can still be made.

Do not flatten the result into “both sides.” A weak claim should remain weak after comparison.

## Pass 4: Adversarial peer review

Review the synthesis as a demanding editor:

- Did any lens receive token treatment while the preferred view received detail?
- Does each important citation support the exact nearby claim?
- Were inconvenient findings, negative results, or base rates omitted?
- Did the writing turn an association, analogy, or incentive into a causal claim?
- Are dates, populations, jurisdictions, and definitions comparable?
- Are confidence and tone proportional to the evidence?
- What is still unknown, and what new source or test would be most informative?

Revise once using the answers. Report material uncertainty instead of hiding it behind smooth prose.

## Essay handoff

When research will support an essay, return:

1. a concise answer to the research question;
2. the contradiction map;
3. an evidence ledger with source, claim, scope, and limitation;
4. a thesis and argument map proportional to the evidence;
5. any claim that still needs verification.

Do not bury the essay's position beneath five equal summaries. The lenses broaden the search; evidence quality determines the synthesis.

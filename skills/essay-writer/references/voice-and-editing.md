# Voice and editing

Use the lightest editing level that fulfils the request. Explicit user instructions and assignment rules override these defaults.

## Editing levels

### Light

Correct distracting grammar, spelling, punctuation, and obvious stiffness. Preserve wording, order, paragraphing, quirks, and length wherever possible. Use for proofreading, sensitive personal writing, and drafts with a strong existing voice.

### Standard

Improve thesis, reasoning, rhythm, clarity, specificity, transitions, and reader trust while preserving the draft's structure and personality. Remove repetition and generic phrasing without replacing every ordinary sentence.

### Deep

Rebuild paragraph order, argument, pacing, opening, ending, and emphasis when the essay's shape is the main problem. Preserve facts, constraints, quotations, citations, and recognisable voice. Disclose major interpretive assumptions after the revised essay.

## Build a voice profile

When the user supplies their own sample, infer only traits supported by it:

- formality and directness;
- warmth, humour, scepticism, urgency, or emotional restraint;
- typical sentence and paragraph length;
- preference for plain, literary, conversational, or specialised vocabulary;
- use of questions, fragments, repetition, direct address, and asides;
- punctuation, spelling variant, and formatting habits;
- how the writer introduces, develops, and closes an idea;
- how strongly the writer states uncertainty and judgement.

Use this priority order when signals conflict:

1. Explicit instructions in the current request
2. The essay currently being written or edited
3. User-provided voice samples
4. Audience, rubric, and genre conventions
5. General natural-writing heuristics

Extract the pattern, not the fingerprint. Do not copy unusual metaphors, catchphrases, anecdotes, or distinctive strings merely to reproduce an identity.

## Natural human voice by default

Every draft and revision should read as one real person writing to a real reader. This applies whether the user supplied a writing sample, an earlier draft, or only facts.

- **Only facts supplied:** write in plain first person where the genre uses it, at the level of the stated writer (for example, an undergraduate applicant). Be concrete and direct, keep claims modest, and do not perform eloquence.
- **An earlier draft supplied:** treat it as the source of facts, stance and useful phrases, not as text that must survive word for word. Keep a sentence when it already reads naturally; rewrite it when it is stiff, formulaic or distractingly ungrammatical.
- Vary sentence length and openings because the thought changes, not for decoration. Let some sentences stay plain.
- Prefer the specific nouns and verbs the facts supply over abstract praise or claims about passion.
- Check the whole text against the formula habits in [pattern-audit.md](pattern-audit.md) before returning it.
- Do not add errors, slang, invented anecdotes or quirks to seem human, and do not promise any detector result.

## Named-person requests

Do not claim to write exactly like a living writer, public figure, teacher, classmate, or other identifiable person. Translate the request into broad traits such as compressed sentences, dry humour, concrete observation, measured argument, or restrained conclusions.

If the user owns the sample and wants continuity with their own writing, retain their strongest existing phrases and stated preferences.

## Preserve identity without stereotyping

- Do not infer age, ethnicity, gender, nationality, disability, education, or personality from grammar or dialect.
- Do not erase dialect, code-switching, or culturally specific phrasing simply to make prose more standard.
- Do not add dialect markers, slang, regional references, or cultural details the user did not provide.
- Correct non-native English only to the degree requested.
- Do not mistake grammatical irregularity for human authorship or polish for AI authorship.

## Revise prose, not personality

- Keep distinctive wording that is clear and doing useful work.
- Preserve deliberate compression, hesitation, humour, repetition, or roughness.
- Remove accidental repetition before deliberate repetition.
- Replace vague abstractions with supported detail.
- Vary rhythm where it has become mechanical, not everywhere.
- Match the writer's appetite for explanation; do not over-explain an implication the reader can carry.
- Keep short material short.

## Adapt for accessibility

When the user asks for plain language or a broad audience:

- lead with the point;
- keep references and pronouns clear;
- define necessary terms and abbreviations at first use;
- use headings and lists only when they improve navigation;
- break genuinely difficult sentences without deleting necessary relationships;
- retain precise terminology when replacing it would distort meaning.

Do not equate accessibility with childish tone, extreme brevity, or removal of nuance.

## Editing notes

Return the revised essay before commentary. Add a short note only for:

- a material interpretive choice;
- a claim or citation that needs verification;
- a quotation or number that may be inconsistent;
- a required personal detail that is missing;
- an assignment constraint the draft does not yet meet.

Do not label the result “humanized” or describe ordinary punctuation changes as detector strategy.
